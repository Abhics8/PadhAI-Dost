PHOTOSYNTHESIS: A STUDY GUIDE

Page 1 — Overview
Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria
convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall balanced
equation is: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2. Photosynthesis takes
place primarily in the leaves, inside organelles called chloroplasts. The green
pigment chlorophyll, located in the thylakoid membranes, absorbs light most
strongly in the blue (around 430 nm) and red (around 660 nm) regions of the
spectrum and reflects green light, which is why leaves appear green.

Page 2 — The Light-Dependent Reactions
The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast.
Light is absorbed by photosystem II (P680) and photosystem I (P700). When light
strikes photosystem II, water molecules are split in a process called photolysis,
releasing oxygen as a byproduct, along with electrons and protons. The electrons
travel down an electron transport chain, and their energy is used to pump protons
into the thylakoid lumen, creating a proton gradient. ATP synthase uses this
gradient to produce ATP through chemiosmosis. Photosystem I re-energizes the
electrons, which ultimately reduce NADP+ to NADPH. The net products of the
light-dependent reactions are ATP, NADPH, and oxygen.

Page 3 — The Calvin Cycle
The Calvin cycle, also called the light-independent reactions, takes place in the
stroma of the chloroplast. It does not require light directly but depends on the
ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent reactions. The cycle has three
phases: carbon fixation, reduction, and regeneration. During carbon fixation, the
enzyme RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called ribulose
bisphosphate (RuBP). The resulting molecule is reduced using ATP and NADPH to form
glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a three-carbon sugar. Some G3P is used to make
glucose, while the rest regenerates RuBP so the cycle can continue. Three turns of
the cycle are needed to produce one molecule of G3P available for glucose synthesis.

Page 4 — Factors Affecting the Rate
Three main environmental factors limit the rate of photosynthesis: light intensity,
carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature. As light intensity increases, the
rate increases until it plateaus at the light saturation point. Carbon dioxide is
often the limiting factor in natural conditions; increasing CO2 concentration
raises the rate up to a point. Temperature affects the enzymes involved: the rate
roughly doubles for every 10 degrees Celsius increase until the optimum (around 25
to 35 degrees Celsius for most plants), after which enzymes such as RuBisCO begin
to denature and the rate falls sharply. The principle that the rate is constrained
by whichever factor is in shortest supply is known as the law of limiting factors.

Page 5 — C4 and CAM Plants
Most plants use the C3 pathway described by the Calvin cycle, but some have evolved
adaptations to reduce photorespiration in hot, dry climates. C4 plants, such as
maize and sugarcane, fix carbon into a four-carbon compound in mesophyll cells
before transferring it to bundle-sheath cells where the Calvin cycle occurs,
concentrating CO2 around RuBisCO. CAM plants, such as cacti and pineapples, open
their stomata at night to fix CO2 into organic acids, then release it for the
Calvin cycle during the day while stomata stay closed to conserve water. Both
adaptations improve water-use efficiency compared with C3 plants.
